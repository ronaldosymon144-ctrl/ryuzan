The trade in one paragraph

You borrow yen at 1%, sell it for dollars, and hold something that pays 3.625%. You clear roughly 2 to 2.5% a year in carry for taking no view on anything. The position is short yen, unhedged by construction, and negatively skewed — many small positive days, rare violent ones. A single 3% move against you erases fourteen months of carry, which is why the unwinds are gap risk rather than trend risk.

Everyone in the trade knows this. What they do not have is a scored, timestamped read on the one actor who can move the price on purpose — Japan's Ministry of Finance — delivered while Tokyo is still trading.

The whole thesis: Japanese policy language, read in the original and scored against a measured history of what followed and against what the market is currently pricing, improves trading and risk decisions. A machine that reads it natively and scores it against that history is a decision advantage on the one variable that moves this price on purpose — tested on whether it improves trading and risk decisions, not on winning a race.

The four steps, using one real sentence

Most people collapse these four into one word — "news" — and then wonder why news does not make money. They are different things, and only the last one is worth anything.

01SIGNALSomething happened At 09:47 Tokyo time a Ministry of Finance official said seven words: あらゆる選択肢を排除しない — "we are not ruling out any options."

That is all a signal is. A thing that occurred, with a timestamp. It carries no opinion and it is worth nothing on its own.
02CONTEXTWhat those words meant last time Those exact words sit on rung 6 of an 8-rung ladder. What followed is on the public record: the ministry disclosed zero intervention for the window ending 29 July, then ¥15.3993 trillion — about $100 billion in the window that opened on 30 July. The largest single deployment in the series.

Context is the memory, and it is dated. A machine that has read every statement since 2022 and holds the disclosure calendar has it; a person reading today's headline does not. Note the discipline: wording and spending are reported on different clocks, and the gap between them is never assumed.
03INTELLIGENCEWhat it is worth right now Rung 6, for the third time this year, with protection still cheap and speculative positioning crowded on one side. The English wires have not carried it yet.

Intelligence is signal plus memory plus today's price. It is the first thing in the chain that could be wrong — which is also the first thing that could be valuable.
04DECISIONWhat you actually do Buy a three-month option that pays if the yen strengthens. Risk 0.6% of the book — $180,000. If it works it returns roughly $740,000. If the rate goes above 158.40 the idea was wrong and the position is closed.

A decision has a size, a price and a point at which you admit you were wrong. Anything without those three is a conversation, not a decision.
The whole business lives in the gap between step 1 and step 4. Everyone gets step 1 eventually — it is public. The advantage is closing the other three steps in minutes rather than the following morning, on the one actor who can move this price on purpose.

The day, on two clocks

Who does what

OSAKA — the Observer

Two hours a day, 09:00 to 11:00 JST. Watches the ministry press pen and the morning wires — the window the measurement says is worth seven hours. Writes down the words that were said, who said them, where it appeared and at what minute, and how it differed from last time.

The whole job in one line: record what was said, exactly, and never say what you think it means.

THE MACHINES — all night, both places

Twenty-four hours, no handover. They poll, read, classify, score and compose. They never sleep, never get bored on the ninth quiet Tuesday in a row, and never decide that this one is probably nothing.

The whole job in one line: turn a sentence into a scored, timestamped, five-line message.

MIAMI — the Approver

Two to five decisions a week, on a phone. Reads five lines. Taps one of three buttons. Nothing reaches the market and nothing reaches a subscriber without that tap.

The whole job in one line: decide, and live with it on the record.

Notice what is not in any of those three jobs. Nobody is watching a screen all day waiting for something to happen. The Observer records but does not judge; the Approver judges but does not monitor; the machines monitor but do not decide. Each one is doing the thing it is actually good at, and nothing else.

The rule that keeps it honest

Every signal is written down before the decision, and kept whichever way the decision goes. Approved, halved, declined — all three land in the same permanent record, with what happened afterwards attached. A track record containing only the trades someone took is advertising. One containing every signal raised and what was done about it is evidence, and evidence is what people pay a subscription for.

What good and bad look like

A good week

Two alerts. One acted on. Neither of them wrong in a way that cost anything. The score rose ahead of the move rather than alongside it, and the Japanese read landed before the English wires by a measurable number of minutes.

Quiet weeks are not failures. Most weeks nothing is close to rung 6, the alert budget goes unspent, and the correct output is silence.

A bad week

Nine alerts. That is the failure mode that actually kills systems like this — not being wrong, but being noisy until the approver stops reading. The budget of two tier-1 alerts a month exists for exactly that reason and it is enforced in code, not in discipline.

The other bad week: the score moved after the price did. That is a lagging indicator wearing a costume, and the replay test on day 7 is designed to catch it before anyone pays for it.

Eight lessons. Nothing assumed. Every number worked through so you can check it yourself.

Lesson 01

The quote is inverted

USD/JPY is quoted as yen per dollar. At 153, one dollar buys 153 yen. So when the number falls, a dollar buys fewer yen — the yen has strengthened.

Check it
You hold $1,000. At 150 → ¥150,000 At 140 → ¥140,000 Fewer yen. The yen won.

Every chart here: line down = yen bid.

Tape goes 160 → 150. Long or short yen wins?

Long yen. It takes fewer yen to buy a dollar, so each yen is worth more. Anyone short yen — which is everyone in the carry trade — is offside.

Lesson 02

The carry trade

Japan's policy rate is 1.00%. The Fed's is around 3.625%. Borrow in the cheap currency, hold the expensive one, collect the spread. That spread is the carry.

One unit of the trade
Borrow ¥100,000,000 ≈ $651,000 at 153.61 Hold USD a year at 3.625% $674,600 Earned $23,600 Owed −$6,510 Net carry $17,089 2.6%

No alpha required. Which is exactly why the position is crowded — thousands of books hold the same risk at the same time.

Lesson 03

Why it gaps

You owe yen and hold dollars. If yen bids, you need more dollars to cover the same notional.

A 3% day
153.61 → 149.00 Cover cost $671,141 Received $650,999 Mark −$20,142 Annual carry was $17,089

One session takes fourteen months of carry. Nobody sits through it — books cut, everyone cuts together, the move extends, the next tier gets stopped. This is gap risk, not trend risk, and a stop inside the range does not fill where you left it.

Lesson 04

Leverage is the amplifier

Currency moves are small, so the trade is run levered. That is the business model, not recklessness — but it converts an ordinary session into a solvency event.

Same 3% move, by gross
1× 3% → −3% of capital 5× 3% → −15% 10× 3% → −30% 20× 3% → −60%

Margin calls arrive for everyone at once. That synchrony is what turns a policy headline into a cascade, and why August 2024 printed the third-largest VIX spike on record.

Lesson 06

Buying the gap instead of wearing it

An option pays if spot passes a strike before expiry and expires worthless otherwise. The premium is the entire risk — you are long convexity rather than short it.

Long yen calls
Premium $180,000 max loss Value on the move $740,000 less premium −$180,000 Net $560,000 3.1 : 1 Breakeven hit rate 180 ÷ (560 + 180) = 24%

You can be wrong three times in four and lose nothing. That is the whole structure — the desk is not trying to be right, it is trying to lift the hit rate from 20% to 33%.

Lesson 07

For every dated policy event, the system reads spot that week, reads it again 1, 4 and 12 weeks later, and takes the percentage change. Nothing modelled.

How this was computed, and what it does not yet include

Method

Each event was placed at its institution's scheduled publication time in JST, converted to UTC, and measured forward to the next 08:00 local open in London and New York — with daylight saving resolved per event date, which moves the answer by a full hour for events between late March and late October.

Coverage: 11 of the 28 events. The seven interventions are excluded because an operation has no scheduled time and is the outcome the system predicts rather than an input to it. Two market events and three bilateral meetings have no fixed schedule and are excluded. Five US events form the control.

What the number covers

This is the structural component: the hours between a Japanese institution publishing and the deeper markets opening. It is arithmetic on published schedules, so it is exact.

The second half of the window is the transmission component — the minutes between a Japanese source carrying a statement and an English wire carrying it. That needs paired publication timestamps from both feeds. The day-4 reader is instrumented to record one every time it classifies, so the desk starts accumulating that measurement from the first week it runs.

This is a staffing map, and that is what makes it useful. It says which two hours of the day are worth a person and which twenty-two are worth a machine. Seven hours on a morning ministry remark is a different product decision from thirty minutes on an afternoon press conference, and until it was measured the desk had no basis for spending its only scarce resource — human attention — on one rather than the other. The claim that has to be earned separately is a decision advantage: that reading this language in the original, against its history and against current pricing, produces better decisions. That is what the instrumentation below is built to test, and it can hold even if the speed advantage turns out to be small.

So the warning and the intervention are two separate trades, different size, different horizon. Conflating them is how a desk sizes the first like the second.

What this page does not claim

These hours are not an information advantage, and this document does not claim one. The measurement covers 11 of the 28 events in the dataset. The seven interventions are excluded because an operation has no scheduled publication time and is the outcome the system predicts rather than an input to it. Only the structural component has been measured. The transmission component — the minutes between a Japanese source carrying a statement and an English wire carrying it — requires paired publication timestamps and has not yet been observed. 3 · Why a yen move shows up in markets that have nothing to do with Japan

Picture several large investors who all borrowed the same cheap currency and bought completely different things with it — one bought Mexican peso deposits, one bought Australian bank shares, one bought an American technology index, one bought a basket of emerging-market bonds. Nothing in those four portfolios overlaps.

What overlaps is the loan. When the yen rises, every one of them owes more than they did yesterday in the currency they have to repay. They all need the same exit at the same time: buy back yen, sell whatever they own that is easiest to sell. The assets are unrelated. The balance-sheet pressure is shared — and that, not any economic connection between Mexico and Australian banks, is why the four move together on the same afternoon.

The design rule that makes the rest of this work. A confirmation is worth having only when it brings a different mechanism. Equity volatility, equity losses and high-yield spreads cannot be counted as three independent votes when all three are simply the same risk shock measured three ways. Nearly every naive scoring system fails here, and it fails in the direction that flatters itself: it produces a high-confidence reading out of one piece of information counted repeatedly.

The pattern holds, weakly, across the wider sample. Specification Every statistic the desk publishes is computed at episode level, not event level, and prints its episode count beside its event count. Three of the five confirmations fall in one week of August 2024, so counting events rather than episodes would hand that single week a fifth of the modern sample. The atlas ships episode identifiers for exactly this reason, and the validation programme clusters on them.
Nearly nine-tenths of Japan's documented intervention history runs in the opposite direction to this product's thesis. For most of the last thirty-five years the Ministry has been a seller of yen, not a buyer: ¥20.4tn across ninety-one days in 2003 alone, ¥14.3tn in 2011 after the earthquake. A model trained on "the Ministry intervened" — without a signed direction field — learns the wrong sign roughly eighty-eight percent of the time, and learns it from the largest and most confident part of the sample. Decision Direction is a required field on every intervention record in the store, and the escalation ladder is defined only over yen-buying operations. What this page does not claim

Profitable tradability is not established here, and the literature does not establish it either. Everything on this page is descriptive co-movement measured in event windows. No causal claim is made, and nothing here has been tested after costs, slippage, capacity or latency.

The sample is small and concentrated. Five events meet the confirmation rule and three of those fall in one week of August 2024. Twenty-six independent episodes underlie forty-five events. Pinning a base rate to within ten percentage points would need roughly eighty-two episodes; there are twenty-six.

Two of the five clusters are not yet instrumented. No source in the current data carries JPY implied volatility, risk reversals, cross-currency basis or repo. The loss budget, at programme level

Capping each trade at 0.6% does not cap the year. Twenty-four full-premium losses under fixed sizing consume 14.4% of the starting book. Per-trade limits are not a risk framework on their own.
Annual premium budget

A hard ceiling on total premium deployed per year, set before the first trade and enforced in code alongside the per-trade limit.

Overlapping position cap

A maximum number of live positions at once. Convexity assumes independent draws; overlapping positions on one thesis are a single larger bet wearing three names.

Drawdown halt

A programme-level loss level at which new positions stop until the replay is re-run. The gate that catches a regime the score no longer describes.

Sakana reads the original and proposes a rung. A Japanese-speaking verifier confirms or corrects it before any signal deploys. The model expedites the interpretation; it does not decide it.

Latency, and what happens when nobody answers

The hours fit

The ministry speaks in the Tokyo morning, and the measured window on that event class is about seven hours before London opens. A verifier working 09:00–11:00 JST is awake precisely when the events that require verification occur.

The 15:30 press conference is the exception, and it is also the event class where the window had already collapsed to thirty minutes — so gating it costs almost nothing that was not already gone.

The budget, stated

Target: verification within 15 minutes during Tokyo hours. Median and 95th-percentile verification latency is measured and published in the weekly scorecard alongside every other clock.

Verification time is one of the six clocks. It is not a hidden delay — it is a reported one, and if it grows the design is wrong.

Nobody answers

The signal does not deploy. After the stated interval it is logged as unverified — not sent, which is recorded as its own outcome and counted against the system, not hidden.

A named alternate verifier covers scheduled absence. There is no automatic release path on timeout — a system that ships unverified signals when the verifier is asleep does not have a verification gate.

The honest trade, named. This inserts minutes between a statement and a signal. Earlier drafts of this document would have called that a cost, because they were arguing about speed. That claim has been retired. The proposition now is decision quality — and a signal a native speaker has confirmed is worth more than the same signal fifteen minutes earlier with nobody's name on it. The latency is reported so the trade can be judged rather than assumed.

The three human hands

THE APPROVER human

Five lines on a phone: what changed, the case, the counter-case, the trade, the invalidation. Approve, decline, or halve. Two to five decisions a week, covering both the position and its publication.

THE OBSERVER human

Osaka, 09:00–11:00 JST — the hours the measurement identifies as the widest window. Records what was said and how it landed on a fixed four-field form. There is no recommendation field.

THE VERIFIER human · Japanese

Confirms or corrects the rung Sakana proposed, reading the original. 10 to 30 items a month, held to a 15-minute target in Tokyo hours. Sees the phrase, the speaker and the setting — not the score, and not the proposed trade.

Blind to the position by design. A verifier who knows the desk is long yen is no longer verifying language.

5 · What agents genuinely replace — and what they do not

The $1.65m staffing line assumes people doing work. Most of that work is production. Some of it is accountability, and accountability does not delegate.

Agents replace this, at Tier 0

Capture — polling twenty sources on their own cadence, stamping arrival, detecting revisions.
Classification — reading the original Japanese, returning a typed object with the triggering phrase.
Comparison — retrieving similar historical episodes and naming the differences.
Challenge — arguing the opposite case on a separate prompt.
Scoring and reporting — arithmetic, the five sub-indices, the daily heartbeats, the weekly scorecard.
Reconciliation — checking the record matches what actually happened.

Agents do not replace this, at any tier

The accountable reviewer. Someone signs. That is the design, not a limitation of the technology — a system where no named person approved the decision has no record worth publishing.

The legal classification. Counsel writes it once, in writing, about the actual product. An agent cannot hold that opinion and an agent cannot be liable for it.

The bilingual grader. Scoring the classifier against a human is the whole validity test. Grading it against another model tests agreement, not truth.

The design-partner conversation. Asking a treasurer what decision they would change is a human conversation, and the answer is the product.

The honest limit of lean. Tier 0 cannot claim returns after implementation cost, cannot sell a licensed product across jurisdictions, and cannot carry a trading book. It is not a smaller version of the institution — it is the instrument that decides whether the institution deserves to exist. Saying so plainly is what makes the small number credible rather than naive.

Why PRICING is separate from RHETORIC. The two disagree more often than you would expect, and the disagreement is the trade. Officials escalating while risk reversals stay cheap means the market has not believed them yet — which is precisely when convexity is worth owning. Officials quiet while protection is bid means someone knows something you do not. A single blended score would hide both.
Which is why a score alone never fires a trade. The decision answers four questions separately — what changed in official intent, how vulnerable positioning is, how much is already priced, and which available action offers an acceptable payoff. A single number crossing a threshold answers none of them.
The ladder itself is a hypothesis under test, not a finding. The rungs are a reconstruction; the ministry publishes no such list. Each classification therefore carries speaker, institutional authority, novelty, repetition, whether the remark was prompted or volunteered, and whether officials subsequently acted — the same phrase carries different information from a different person in a different setting. Whether the ladder predicts anything is a separate question from whether the model reads it correctly, and only the second is settled on day 5.
And intervention is one channel, not the whole mechanism. Yen funding conditions also move through BOJ policy, US rates, funding stress and portfolio reallocation — and the BIS cautions that total cross-border yen borrowing cannot simply be read as carry-trade exposure, because what the borrowing is used for is not directly observable. Treating every yen move as an intervention story would overfit the ladder to one cause.
This argues for a larger product than the one first described. Japan policy and funding-risk intelligence, with carry-trade unwinds as the first application — more durable than a single-trade monitor, and a closer description of what a Japanese business-intelligence company actually does.

7 · The implementation

One job, defined narrowly enough to be graded: classify a Japanese official text against a versioned ladder. Not translate. Not summarise. Not conclude.

The ladder is a file

Eight rungs, each with its canonical phrases, kept as ladder.v3.json — not baked into a prompt. Revising it bumps the version and re-runs the whole history, so every score in the series was produced by a ladder you can point at.

Typed output, not prose

Structured-output schema on every call. The model returns an object or the call fails — there is no free-text response to parse, and therefore no parser to break at 09:47 on a Tuesday.

Drops into the same client

OpenAI-compatible endpoint — a base-URL and a key, the same code path as every other model call.

Benchmarked, not assumed: scored against a multilingual frontier model and a plain rules baseline on the same set, measuring false escalations, missed escalations, evidence accuracy, latency and human correction time. Its investor register is background, not evidence of superiority on this task.

The call contract
IN { text: full Japanese original, untouched speaker: 財務官 | 日銀総裁 | 財務大臣 | … setting: press pen | conference | Diet | release published: ISO timestamp, JST ladder: v3 } OUT { rung: 1–8 confidence: 0.00–1.00 trigger_phrase: the exact Japanese that decided it gloss: literal English, for the record only novelty: first_use | repeat | softened ladder_version: v3 model_version: pinned } VERIFY { verified_rung: set by the human, or null verified_by: initials + timestamp verify_latency: seconds from publication agreed: true | false }
trigger_phrase is the load-bearing field. It is what makes the judgement checkable six months later: a human can read the phrase the model acted on and say whether it deserved rung 6, without re-reading the document or trusting the score.

Grading it — and what happens when the two readers disagree

The golden set became continuous

The day-5 blind grading still happens — it is the screen that says whether the ladder is even coherent. But once a verifier is standing, the golden set stops being a sample and becomes a census.

Every escalation-relevant document yields a labelled pair: model rung, verified rung, and the phrase both were reading. After a year that is a confusion matrix on this ladder and this document distribution — a dataset that cannot be bought and that no competitor holds.

The day-5 screen

Fifty historical statements graded blind by a bilingual reader on day 5. Acceptance: exact-rung agreement at or above 70%, within one rung at or above 90%. Below that the ladder is wrong before the model is.

Fifty is the day-5 screen, not the operational bar. Before the classifier is relied on it expands well beyond fifty and must include negation, quotations, repeated boilerplate, ambiguous phrasing and genuine changes in policy stance — the cases where a confident wrong answer costs most.

Disagreement is data

Both readers classify the same event. When the Japanese read lands two or more rungs above the English read, that gap is itself the signal — it means the escalation survived in the original and was flattened in translation.

Those cases go straight to the top of the Observer's queue, because they are where a human eye is worth the most.

No fallback to translation. If the endpoint is unavailable the document is marked unread and the desk is paged. It is never re-routed through an English translation, because a translated read is a different measurement — mixing the two would quietly corrupt the one series the whole system is judged on.

What Sakana is deliberately not asked to do

Not thisBecause
Score, weight or rankThe score must be reproducible forever from fixed arithmetic. A model that improves cannot be allowed to silently re-price history.
Write the case or the tradeClassification and conclusion are separate jobs held by separate components, so a bad conclusion never contaminates a good reading.
Read the English wiresThe English path exists to be compared against. If the same model ran both sides there would be no lead time to measure.
Write into the canonical logModel output lands in a derived table. Re-running a new version rebuilds the derived layer and leaves the source record untouched.
Decide the rung that shipsIt proposes; a Japanese speaker disposes. The model expedites interpretation — compressing hours of reading into seconds — but the rung that reaches a signal is the one a human confirmed. Both are stored, and the gap between them is measured.

9 · The chain, end to end

01continuousCollect Scheduled functions poll every source on its own cadence. Each writes raw, stamps its fetch time, and never overwrites.
02+2 secDedupe and route Hash on canonical URL and first 200 characters. Detect language. Japanese goes left, English goes right.
03+8 secClassify Sakana returns {rung, confidence, trigger_phrase, gloss, speaker, setting} as a typed object. The English path runs the same schema on a general model. Both land in the derived table with a model version attached.
04+1 secScore Five sub-indices recomputed in code. Stored with the code version. No model touches this step.
05instantThreshold Compare against the calibrated levels and the alert budget. Most changes stop here, silently, which is the point.

Three constraints shape this one. The rules the message set obeys

One asking message

Only the event alert carries buttons. If a message has no buttons it needs nothing from you, and that is learnable in a week.

Same shape every time

The field labels never move. The approver reads position, not prose — which is what makes a nine-second read possible at all.

Heartbeats are unconditional

Pre-open and overnight go out on quiet days too. A missing heartbeat pages the desk before a human ever notices.

The budget is a noise target, not a gate

Two tier-1 alerts a month governs discretionary alerts. Urgent position-risk alerts always reach the approver — an invalidation level breached, a position moving against the book, a gap through a stop. Suppressing those to protect a noise metric is how a risk system kills someone.

Numbers carry their age

Every figure prints the time it was true of. A stale number that looks live is worse than no number.

12 · The notification layer

Telegram — the decision channel

Bot API. Free, no message templates to clear, and inline keyboards that post the answer straight back to a webhook — so approval is one tap, not a reply that has to be parsed.

sendMessage with reply_markup out; callback_query in. The approval, the approver and the timestamp are written against the signal before anything publishes.

Also the distribution channel. Tiered channels, subscribers added and removed by the platform's own auth. No app to build.

WhatsApp — the reach channel

Meta Cloud API. Business-initiated messages need a pre-approved template, which suits a fixed five-line alert format precisely — the template is written once and the variables are filled per signal.

Service conversations are free within the 24-hour window after a subscriber writes in; business-initiated conversations are priced per conversation by country.

Use it for reach, not for approval. Where the audience already lives, it arrives; where a decision is needed, Telegram's buttons are faster.

Controls that sit after the approval tap

Code-based risk limits and human approval are necessary and not sufficient. Five more controls sit between the tap and the market.

Quote expiry

The price in the alert has a life. Past it the signal is re-priced or dropped — an approval tapped forty minutes late must not execute against a stale level.

Duplicate-order prevention

One approval, one order. A re-tap, a retried webhook or a second device cannot produce a second position.

Risk recheck after approval

The gate runs again on the approved size at the current price, not the price that produced the alert. A halved size is re-gated as a new order.

Execution reconciliation

What was intended, what filled, and at what price — reconciled and written to the record. The published track record reports fills, not intentions.

Approver unavailable

A defined response when nobody taps: a named alternate, an automatic decline after a stated interval, and the non-decision recorded as its own outcome.

8 · Build notes

Responsive

Single column below 700px. Tab rail scrolls horizontally. Charts sit in overflow-x:auto containers with a min-width; the body never scrolls sideways.

Accessibility

Tabs use role="tablist" with arrow-key navigation. Bubbles are focusable buttons with labels. Every status colour is paired with a word or shape, never colour alone.

Performance

No libraries. One font request. The chart redraws imperatively on filter change — 141 points and 28 bubbles, so no virtualisation needed.

Extending it

To add a market: extend fx_weekly with a series key, add its events, and add a selector. The scorer and backtest logic are series-agnostic.
